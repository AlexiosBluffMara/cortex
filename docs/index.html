The Brain Cinema — how Cortex works

Picture a movie theater. Your brain is the audience. Every seat in that theater corresponds to one tiny patch of your brain's outer surface — the cortex. Cortex (the tool) is the camera system and the film critic rolled into one.

The movie is whatever you upload: a short video clip, an audio recording, an image, or a block of text.

The audience is your brain — 20,484 people in 20,484 assigned seats, each responsible for a specific job: seeing faces, recognizing voices, feeling suspense, processing language. When something interesting happens on screen, certain sections lean forward. Blood rushes to those seats. That rush of blood is called the BOLD signal — the audience's live reaction meter.

TRIBE v2 is the high-speed sensor system in every seat. Trained by Meta on 25 human subjects, it watches the movie at the front of the theater and predicts, twice per second, how excited each audience member is going to get — 3.5 to 5 seconds before their reaction visibly peaks. (Biology is slow: when neurons fire, the blood that feeds them takes a few seconds to arrive. Cortex has already corrected for it.)

Gemma 4 is the film critic in the back booth. After the screening, Gemma reads the audience-reaction printout and explains what happened — at three levels of detail, depending on whom the explanation is for.

"Twenty thousand seats. One movie. One critic. About six minutes."

The three narration tiers

Three different film critics writing about the same screening. Same facts. Different reader. Pick the one that fits the audience.

tier · general
For a curious high-schooler
"The audience in the back-left section lit up — those are the people responsible for recognizing faces. A close-up just appeared on screen, and 3.5 seconds later that section leaned forward in their seats."
tier · college
For someone who took bio or psych
"Primary visual and fusiform face regions showed strong bilateral activation within the visual network (peak z = 4.2 at t = 7), consistent with face-selective response in the ventral visual stream."
tier · clinical
For a clinician or researcher
"Yeo-7 Visual network activation (bilateral occipital, fusiform): peak BOLD at t = 7 (3.5 s post-stimulus, 5 s HRF lag pre-applied). Left-lateralized superior temporal gyrus response suggests auditory-linguistic processing."

Why it matters medically

A clinical fMRI session — a real brain scan in a real hospital — costs between $3,000 and $6,000, takes 90 minutes, and requires a radiologist to interpret. That price tag puts brain science out of reach for most of the world.

Cortex does not replace a clinical scan. It is not a diagnostic tool. Its predictions are averaged across 25 subjects, not tuned to your individual brain. But it opens research doors that are currently locked:

  • Educational neuroscience. Which brain networks does a given lecture, training video, or online course actually engage? Educators can iterate on content the way product designers iterate on interfaces — with signal, not guesswork.
  • Rehabilitation research. Does a therapy video reach the target motor or language regions? Quantify it without booking a scanner.
  • Media and accessibility. Which cognitive systems does a piece of content activate? Designers, filmmakers, and accessibility engineers can answer this question for the first time without a neuroscience lab.

The hardware that runs this costs around $2,000 at MSRP — street price $2,500–3,000 for the RTX 5090. The equivalent cloud capacity on a Google Cloud L4 GPU costs about $0.70/hour, or roughly $504/month running 24/7. At scale-to-zero it approaches $0. The direction is unmistakable.

What Cortex is not

  • Not a diagnostic tool. Nothing Cortex produces should be used to diagnose, treat, or screen for any medical condition.
  • Not a personal brain scan. TRIBE v2 is trained on 25 subjects and outputs a population-averaged prediction. It predicts how a statistically average human brain responds to your video — not how your specific brain responds.
  • Not a replacement for fMRI. Clinical fMRI measures actual blood flow in your brain. Cortex predicts what blood flow would look like, based on a model trained on a small group of people watching TV shows and movies.
  • Not subcortical. The predictions cover the cortical surface only — the outer rind of the brain. Deep structures like the amygdala, hippocampus, and thalamus are not modeled.
  • Not trained on medical data. TRIBE v2 was trained on subjects watching the TV series Friends and feature films, not on patients or clinical stimuli.

Architecture

The pipeline, end to end:

Upload
  └─ media_gate          Gemma 4 multimodal — describes what it sees
       └─ TRIBE v2        Predicts (T × 20,484) BOLD z-scores at 2 Hz
            └─ BrainAnalysis   Schaefer-400 parcellation, Yeo-7 networks, top ROIs
                 └─ Gemma narrate × 3 tiers   General · College · Clinical
                      └─ WebSocket → Three.js viewer   Per-vertex 3D animation

On a 32 GB card (RTX 5090 baseline), the GPU scheduler swaps TRIBE v2 (~22.4 GB) and Gemma 4 E4B (~10 GB) sequentially — they cannot coexist on the same device, so swap takes about 10 seconds per cycle. On enterprise hardware (A100 80 GB, H100 80 GB, L40S 48 GB), both models can stay resident simultaneously — no swap, end-to-end latency drops by ~30 %. The state machine is simple: IDLE → GEMMA_ACTIVE → TRIBE_ACTIVE, with eviction-driven swaps and a Google Cloud Run L4 fallback when the local GPU is busy.

Where Cortex runs — from RTX 5090 to ACCESS HPC

The RTX 5090 is the baseline, not the ceiling. Cortex was tuned for Blackwell sm_120, but the model graph and inference pipeline are architecture-agnostic — every modern NVIDIA accelerator above the 5090 is faster, cheaper-per-job, or both. We're targeting deployment on national HPC resources via ACCESS-CI through partner institutions.

The deployable container we ship as a GitHub release bakes in the model weights and the runtime so the operator can docker load on a fresh node and have the pipeline live in minutes — useful for air-gapped HPC environments where outbound HuggingFace downloads aren't permitted. See the release notes for SHAs and the offline bootstrap procedure.

Run it locally

Hardware: RTX 5090 (32 GB) is the baseline. For better performance, scale up to enterprise cards (RTX 6000 Ada, L40S, A100, H100). HPC users on ACCESS-CI at UIUC and similar facilities can run the full pipeline on a single A100/H100 with both models resident — no swap, faster end-to-end.

Other prerequisites: Python 3.11+, Ollama, and ~30 GB of disk space for model weights (TRIBE v2 from HuggingFace + Gemma 4 from Ollama).

# 1. Clone Cortex
git clone https://github.com/AlexiosBluffMara/cortex
cd cortex

# 2. Make a Python environment
python -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate    # Windows: .venv\Scripts\activate
pip install -e ".[dev]"

# 3. Pull the fast narration model
ollama pull gemma4:e4b

# 4. Start the server
uvicorn webapp.server:app --host 0.0.0.0 --port 8765
# Open http://localhost:8765
